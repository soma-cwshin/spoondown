Evidence

What the app is based on

Spoon Down borrows a few ideas from research. Here is what each feature uses, what the studies showed, and where they fall short.

Spoon Down is a wellness app, not a medical device. It does not diagnose or treat anything, and it does not promise weight loss. If eating feels out of control or you are worried about your health, please talk to a health professional. The app’s Settings list places to get help.

  • Body-state clock

    In the app: the time since your last meal is shown as four states: Digesting · Diet mode · Fat-burn focus (from 12 hours) · Long fast (from 16 hours). The app does not recommend fasting longer than 16 hours.

    What research shows: a 2018 review describes a “metabolic switch”: typically beyond 12 hours after you stop eating, the liver’s glycogen runs low and the body leans more on fat for fuel. The app uses the early end of that range.

    Limits: the timing differs from person to person and depends on what and how much you ate and how active you were. The app’s times are general estimates for reference only.

    Anton SD et al. Flipping the Metabolic Switch: Understanding and Applying the Health Benefits of Fasting. Obesity, 2018. PubMed

  • Craving SOS: 15-min hold and 3-minute mini-games

    In the app: when a craving hits, you hold for 15 minutes with one small thing to do. The suggested first task is a 3-minute mini-game you play with one button.

    What research shows: food images that pop into your head feed a craving, and a short task that keeps your eyes and hands busy can push them out. In a 2014 lab study at the University of Plymouth, people who played Tetris for 3 minutes reported cravings about 24% weaker than people who watched a loading screen. In a 2015 real-world study, 3 minutes of Tetris lowered craving strength by about 14 points on a 0–100 scale, for food and other cravings, and the effect held across the week.

    Limits: both studies were small and used Tetris, not our games. Our mini-games are original one-button games built on the same idea (keep eyes and hands busy); they have not been tested in a study. The 15 minutes is the app’s choice of a short, doable wait, not a number taken from these studies.

    Tetris can diminish cravings (University of Plymouth, Appetite, 2014). Medical Xpress
    Skorka-Brown J, Andrade J, Whalley B, May J. Playing Tetris decreases drug and other cravings in real world settings. Addictive Behaviors, 2015. University of Plymouth

  • Rotten Delivery

    In the app: you can “order” the food you’re craving for nothing. It arrives rotten, and what you would have spent is added to Money saved. The rotten picture is off unless you turn it on.

    What research shows: in experiments by Hollands and colleagues, pairing pictures of snacks with pictures of negative health consequences made people choose fruit over snacks more often in the lab.

    Limits: these were short lab effects, and the studies used health-consequence images, not rotten food. That is why the picture is optional and the feature is meant to be light and funny rather than a treatment.

    Hollands GJ, Prestwich A, Marteau TM. Using aversive images to enhance healthy food choices and implicit attitudes. Health Psychology, 2011. PubMed
    Hollands GJ, Marteau TM. Pairing images of unhealthy and healthy foods with images of negative and positive health consequences. Health Psychology, 2016. University of Cambridge

  • Craving hour reminders

    In the app: from your own records over the last 14 days, the app looks for a one-hour window when cravings showed up on at least 3 days, and checks in about 30 minutes before it.

    What research shows: OnTrack, a research app, tried to predict dietary lapses and step in before they happened. In its first open trial with 43 adults, people used the prompts and reported fewer unplanned lapses.

    Limits: OnTrack was a small, early study using machine learning and many daily questions. Spoon Down uses a much simpler rule on your own records, and its reminders have not been tested in a study.

    Forman EM et al. OnTrack: development and feasibility of a smartphone app designed to predict and prevent dietary lapses. Translational Behavioral Medicine, 2019. PubMed
